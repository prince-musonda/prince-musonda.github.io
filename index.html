Essay · Efficient AI · BitNet

I Have a Dream. A Dream of Sustainable and Efficient AI.

A dream where every independent scientist and engineer can experiment, explore, and train AI models without worrying about enormous computational costs, expensive hardware, or energy demands.

Two areas of research that particularly excite me are BitNet and alternatives to backpropagation.

I have a dream. A dream of sustainable and efficient AI.

A dream where every independent scientist and engineer can experiment, explore, and train AI models to solve real-world problems without worrying about enormous computational costs, expensive hardware, or energy demands.

A dream where the ability to innovate in AI is not determined by how many GPUs you can afford or how much funding you have.

Two areas of research that particularly excite me are BitNet and alternatives to backpropagation.

Much of what I find exciting about these ideas comes from the work of very smart people who are challenging how we think about building and training neural networks.

So, let us talk about BitNet.

The idea is based on a simple question: what if we could train and build huge models that use only -1, 0, and 1 as their weights?

Yes, I know it sounds weird, but research has shown that ternary-weight models can achieve competitive performance compared with conventional models of similar scale.

But why would I want to build a model that only uses three types of numbers in the first place?

Think about it.

If your model uses only three possible weight values, then each number can be represented using just two bits instead of 16 or 32 bits.

You could represent 0 using 00, 1 using 01, and -1 using 10. The precise encoding is up to you.

In a standard 16-bit block of memory, where you would normally store one weight such as 2.3 or 4, you could instead store eight ternary weights.

Consider GPT-3, which had around 175 billion parameters and would require about 350 GB of memory to store its weights at 16-bit precision.

If an equivalent model used two bits for every weight, the theoretical weight storage would be approximately 43.75 GB.

Look at that difference.

A 12-billion-parameter model using two-bit weights would theoretically need just 3 GB of memory for its weights, compared with 24 GB for a traditional 16-bit model.

That's eight times less weight memory!

Of course, this doesn't account for all the other memory requirements of running a language model, including activations and key-value caches.

But imagine how much more accessible AI could become if we could reduce hardware requirements this significantly.

And yes, feel free to send me a better computer or a free GPU. I don't mind. 😆🤣

And here's the kicker.

Because the weights are restricted to -1, 0, and 1, multiplication by these weights can be simplified.

If the weight is 1, add the associated input.

If it's -1, subtract the input.

If it's 0, skip it.

This can reduce multiplication operations and potentially make computation more efficient when supported by suitable hardware and optimized implementations.

Ternary neural networks like BitNet are really interesting, but one major challenge remains: the training process.

Efficient inference is only part of the problem. Training large neural networks still requires significant computation and memory.

That's one of the reasons I'm also interested in alternatives to backpropagation and new approaches to training neural networks.

Essay · Neural Networks · BitNet

Why BitNet Works: A Simple House Price Analogy

What if a neural network could learn useful relationships using only three possible weights: -1, 0, and 1?

Let's explore the idea using something familiar: predicting the price of a house.

So, I recently shared a thought on BitNet, a different way of thinking about how we train, build, and design neural networks.

Something I believe could be instrumental in making AI and neural networks more energy efficient, sustainable, and accessible.

Something I believe can help reduce the growing demand for memory chips, especially with the global memory chip shortage we're currently experiencing.

You can read more about it in my previous post ↗ .

But what I wanted to share was an analogy on why it works.

Imagine you want to create a model that can predict the price of a house given its age, size, whether it has a swimming pool or not (1 or 0), and the name of the person who owns it.

When a house gets too old, its price generally goes down. Newer houses tend to be more expensive than older ones.

So, for simplicity, let's say the age of the house has a negative relationship with its price, and we give it an associated weight of -1.

The size of the house generally has a positive relationship with its price. The bigger the house, the higher the price tends to be.

So, let's give this feature an associated weight of 1.

Having a swimming pool can also increase the price of a house.

Let's say we represent this feature with 1 if the house has a pool and 0 if it doesn't (binary encoding).

We can give this feature an associated weight of 1. If the house has a swimming pool, the input is 1, so we add 1. If it doesn't have a pool, the input is 0, so we add nothing.

From this, we can see how a weight of 1 can contribute positively when a swimming pool is present and do nothing when it isn't.

Now, this is my favorite part.

One of the features contains information about the name of the person who owns the house.

For our example, let's assume this information has nothing useful to contribute to predicting the price.

So, what do you think the associated weight could be?

0. Yes, zero!

Meaning, skip this input. It doesn't contribute anything to our output.

Now imagine repeating these simple operations across many layers, with the model learning how to combine and transform information along the way.

These operations of addition, subtraction, and skipping can contribute to a final prediction like $100k for one house and $20.5k for another.

Of course, this is a simplified analogy.

Real BitNet models learn their weights during training, and the relationships between inputs and outputs are much more complex.
